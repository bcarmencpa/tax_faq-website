Business & Self-Employment

Schedule C, entity returns, payroll, and gig economy taxes

11 topics

Schedule C (Profit or Loss from Business) allows deductions for ordinary and necessary business expenses, including:

  • Advertising & marketing — website, business cards, ads
  • Vehicle expenses — mileage (67¢/mile for 2024) or actual costs
  • Home office — simplified ($5/sq ft, up to 300 sq ft) or actual expenses
  • Supplies & materials consumed in your business
  • Professional fees — accountants, attorneys, consultants
  • Insurance — business liability, professional indemnity
  • Education & training to maintain or improve current skills
  • Software & subscriptions used for business
  • Section 179 / Bonus depreciation for equipment and qualified property
  • Health insurance premiums (deducted on Schedule 1 if self-employed)
  • Retirement plan contributions (SEP-IRA, Solo 401k)
Keep receipts and records for all deductions. The expense must be both ordinary (common in your field) and necessary (helpful and appropriate).

Self-employment (SE) tax covers Social Security and Medicare for self-employed individuals. Employees split these taxes with their employer; self-employed individuals pay both sides.

  • Rate: 15.3% on net self-employment income (12.4% Social Security + 2.9% Medicare)
  • SE income base: 92.35% of net profit (you deduct the "employer" half before calculating)
  • Social Security wage base (2025): $176,100 — only earnings up to this are subject to 12.4%
  • Medicare: 2.9% applies to all SE income; additional 0.9% if over $200,000 (single)
  • Deduction: You can deduct half of the SE tax above-the-line

Use our SE Tax Calculator for a quick estimate.

The Qualified Business Income (QBI) deduction (Section 199A) allows eligible self-employed individuals and pass-through business owners to deduct up to 20% of qualified business income.

  • Applies to sole proprietors, S-corps, partnerships, and some trusts
  • Deduction cannot exceed 20% of (taxable income minus net capital gains)
  • For incomes above $197,300 (single) / $394,600 (MFJ) in 2025, complex W-2 wage and property limitations apply
  • Specified Service Trades or Businesses (SSTBs) — health, law, consulting, financial services, performing arts — phase out at the same thresholds
This deduction is currently set to expire after 2025 unless Congress extends it. Contact our office to discuss planning before year-end.

You must pay estimated taxes if you expect to owe at least $1,000 after subtracting withholding and credits. The 2026 payment schedule:

  • Q1 (Jan 1 – Mar 31): Due April 15, 2026
  • Q2 (Apr 1 – May 31): Due June 16, 2026
  • Q3 (Jun 1 – Aug 31): Due September 15, 2026
  • Q4 (Sep 1 – Dec 31): Due January 15, 2027

Safe harbor rules (avoid underpayment penalty): Pay at least 100% of prior year tax (110% if prior year AGI exceeded $150,000), OR 90% of current year tax.

Use Form 1040-ES to calculate and submit payments, or pay online at IRS Direct Pay (irs.gov/payments).

  • Simplified method: $5 per square foot of business space, up to 300 sq ft (max $1,500 deduction). Easy, no depreciation recapture.
  • Regular (actual expense) method: Calculate the business-use percentage of your home (sq ft of office ÷ total sq ft) and apply to actual expenses: rent/mortgage interest, utilities, insurance, repairs, depreciation. More complex but potentially higher deduction.
W-2 employees working from home cannot claim the home office deduction — it was suspended for employees under the 2017 tax law.

Two methods for deducting business vehicle use:

  • Standard mileage rate: 67¢ per mile for 2024 business miles (rate is set annually by the IRS). Simple; multiply business miles by the rate.
  • Actual expense method: Deduct the business-use percentage of gas, insurance, repairs, registration, depreciation, and lease payments.

You must choose the standard mileage method in the first year the vehicle is used for business if you want to use it in later years. Keep a mileage log with date, destination, business purpose, and miles for each trip.

Commuting miles (home to regular work location) are never deductible. Business miles are — e.g., driving to a client site or between business locations.

The IRS standard mileage rates for 2025:

  • Business: 70¢ per mile (up from 67¢ in 2024)
  • Medical / moving (active military only): 21¢ per mile
  • Charitable: 14¢ per mile (set by statute, unchanged for years)

Multiply your total business miles driven in 2025 by $0.70 to get your deduction using the standard rate.

Keep a contemporaneous mileage log. Apps like MileIQ or Everlance make this easy and provide audit-ready reports.

Classification affects taxes significantly. The IRS looks at three categories:

  • Behavioral control: Does the company control how, when, and where work is done?
  • Financial control: Can the worker profit or lose money? Do they have multiple clients? Do they provide their own tools?
  • Type of relationship: Is there a written contract? Are there employee benefits? Is the relationship permanent?

Employees receive W-2; employer withholds income tax, Social Security, Medicare. Independent contractors receive Form 1099-NEC; they pay SE tax and handle their own estimated payments.

Misclassifying employees as contractors is a major IRS audit trigger and can result in significant back taxes and penalties. When in doubt, call us.

You must issue Form 1099-NEC to any non-employee individual or unincorporated business you paid $600 or more during the year for services in the course of your business.

  • Due date: January 31 (both to the recipient and to the IRS)
  • Exceptions: Payments to C-corps and S-corps generally don't require a 1099-NEC (with a few exceptions like attorneys)
  • Required info: Collect Form W-9 from vendors before paying — it captures their name, address, and TIN
  • Penalties for failing to file: $60–$630 per form depending on how late
Also watch for Form 1099-K if you accept payments through PayPal, Venmo, Square, or credit cards. The $600 threshold was being phased in — confirm current IRS guidance.

Converting a sole proprietorship or single-member LLC to an S-Corp can reduce self-employment taxes once net profit exceeds roughly $40,000–$50,000/year. How it works:

  • The owner pays themselves a reasonable salary (subject to payroll taxes)
  • Remaining profit is distributed as dividends (not subject to SE tax)
  • Example: $100,000 profit, $60,000 salary → SE tax only on $60,000 instead of $100,000 → saves ~$6,000

Costs to consider: Payroll setup and processing, annual S-Corp tax return (Form 1120-S), state filing fees, higher accounting costs. These often run $2,000–$4,000/year.

S-Corp elections must be made by March 15 (for calendar-year S-Corps) to be effective for the current tax year. Call us to run the numbers for your situation.

All gig income is taxable — regardless of whether you receive a Form 1099. Common platforms and what to expect:

  • Rideshare (Uber, Lyft): 1099-K for rides + 1099-NEC for incentives. Deduct mileage, phone, water for passengers.
  • Delivery (DoorDash, Instacart, Amazon Flex): 1099-NEC or 1099-K. Deduct mileage, insulated bags, phone.
  • Rental (Airbnb, VRBO): 1099-K for amounts over thresholds. May be on Schedule E (passive) or Schedule C depending on services provided.
  • Freelance/consulting (Upwork, Fiverr): 1099-NEC if over $600. Report on Schedule C.

All gig income flows to Schedule C and is subject to both income tax and self-employment tax. Pay quarterly estimated taxes to avoid penalties.
