Form 1040 — Individual Returns

Filing status, deductions, credits, retirement accounts, healthcare, education & more

23 topics

Your filing status depends on your marital and family situation on December 31 of the tax year. There are five options:

  • Single — Unmarried, divorced, or legally separated.
  • Married Filing Jointly (MFJ) — Generally the most beneficial for married couples; combines income and deductions.
  • Married Filing Separately (MFS) — Sometimes advantageous if one spouse has high medical or miscellaneous expenses, or for legal reasons. Many credits phase out faster.
  • Head of Household (HOH) — Unmarried and paid more than half the cost of keeping up a home for a qualifying person. Offers higher deductions and lower rates than Single.
  • Qualifying Surviving Spouse — If your spouse died in the prior 2 years and you have a dependent child, you can use MFJ rates for 2 years after death.
Tip: If you're unsure, MFJ usually results in the lowest tax for married couples. We can run a comparison for you — call our office.

For tax year 2025, the standard deduction amounts are:

  • Single / Married Filing Separately: $15,000
  • Married Filing Jointly / Qualifying Surviving Spouse: $30,000
  • Head of Household: $22,500

Additional amounts if you are 65 or older or legally blind: +$1,600 (single/HOH) or +$1,300 (MFJ) per qualifying condition.

The standard deduction automatically reduces your taxable income. About 90% of taxpayers use it rather than itemizing.

Itemize only if your total Schedule A deductions exceed your standard deduction. Common itemized deductions include:

  • Mortgage interest on up to $750,000 of acquisition debt
  • State and local taxes (SALT) — capped at $10,000 ($5,000 MFS)
  • Charitable contributions to qualifying organizations
  • Medical expenses exceeding 7.5% of AGI
  • Casualty losses from federally declared disasters

Since the 2017 Tax Cuts and Jobs Act nearly doubled the standard deduction, most taxpayers — especially those without large mortgages or high SALT — benefit from the standard deduction.

Homeowners with big mortgages, high property taxes, or large charitable contributions are the most likely candidates for itemizing.

Schedule A covers the following itemized deductions:

  • Medical & Dental Expenses: Amount exceeding 7.5% of your AGI
  • State & Local Taxes (SALT): Income or sales tax + property taxes, capped at $10,000
  • Mortgage Interest: On up to $750,000 of qualified home loans (up to two homes)
  • Charitable Contributions: Cash up to 60% of AGI; appreciated property up to 30%
  • Casualty & Theft Losses: Only for federally declared disaster areas

The Child Tax Credit (CTC) provides up to $2,000 per qualifying child under age 17. Key rules:

  • Child must have a valid Social Security number
  • Must live with you for more than half the year
  • Up to $1,700 is refundable (Additional Child Tax Credit) even if you owe no tax
  • Phases out at $200,000 AGI (single) / $400,000 AGI (MFJ) — reduces by $50 per $1,000 over the threshold
There is also a $500 non-refundable credit for other dependents who don't qualify for the full CTC (e.g., older children, elderly parents).

The EITC is a refundable credit for low-to-moderate income workers. For 2025, maximum credit amounts:

  • No qualifying children: up to $632
  • One child: up to $4,213
  • Two children: up to $6,960
  • Three or more children: up to $7,830

You must have earned income (wages or self-employment). Investment income must be under $11,600. Income limits vary by filing status and number of children.

Yes — you can deduct up to $2,500 of student loan interest as an above-the-line deduction (no itemizing required).

  • Phases out for single filers with MAGI $75,000–$90,000
  • Phases out for MFJ filers with MAGI $155,000–$185,000
  • Not available if MFS or if you can be claimed as a dependent
  • Interest must be on a qualified student loan for higher education

Your lender will send Form 1098-E showing the interest paid.

You'll receive Form 1099-R for distributions from traditional IRAs, 401(k)s, pensions, and other retirement accounts.

  • Traditional IRA / 401(k): Taxable as ordinary income
  • Roth IRA: Tax-free if you're 59½+ and the account is 5+ years old
  • Early withdrawal (under 59½): Subject to 10% penalty plus regular income tax (exceptions apply for disability, first-time home purchase, substantially equal payments, and others)
  • Required Minimum Distributions (RMDs): Must begin at age 73; failure to take results in 25% excise tax on the shortfall

For tax year 2025:

  • Annual IRA limit: $7,000 ($8,000 if age 50 or older)
  • Roth IRA income phase-out (single): $150,000–$165,000 MAGI
  • Roth IRA income phase-out (MFJ): $236,000–$246,000 MAGI
  • Traditional IRA deductibility: Fully deductible if no workplace plan. If you have a plan, phase-out starts at $79,000 (single) / $126,000 (MFJ)
Deadline: You have until April 15, 2026 to make 2025 IRA contributions.

Yes — HSA contributions are deductible above-the-line (no itemizing required). For 2025:

  • Self-only HDHP coverage: $4,300 contribution limit
  • Family HDHP coverage: $8,550 contribution limit
  • Catch-up (age 55+): Additional $1,000

You must be enrolled in a High-Deductible Health Plan (HDHP) to contribute. HSAs offer a triple tax advantage: contributions are deductible, growth is tax-free, and withdrawals for qualified medical expenses are tax-free.

If you purchased health insurance through the Marketplace (healthcare.gov) and your income is 100%–400% of the Federal Poverty Level (or above if the enhancement is extended), you may qualify for the Premium Tax Credit (PTC).

  • Can be taken in advance to lower monthly premiums (APTC)
  • Reconciled on Form 8962 when you file
  • If advance credits exceed your actual PTC, you must repay the difference (subject to caps)
  • If your actual PTC exceeds advance payments, you get the difference as a refund or credit
Report any changes in income or family size to healthcare.gov during the year to keep advance payments accurate.

Two main education credits — you can only claim one per eligible student per year:

  • American Opportunity Credit (AOC): Up to $2,500/year for the first 4 years of college. 40% refundable. Income phase-out: $80,000–$90,000 single / $160,000–$180,000 MFJ.
  • Lifetime Learning Credit (LLC): Up to $2,000/year (20% of first $10,000 expenses) for any post-secondary education or job-skills courses. Not refundable. Same phase-out ranges as AOC.

Both require Form 1098-T from the educational institution. Cannot be claimed if you use tax-free 529 distributions for the same expenses.

529 plan distributions are reported to you on Form 1099-Q. You (or the designated beneficiary) receive this form.

  • Qualified distributions (tuition, fees, books, room & board, required supplies) are completely tax-free
  • K-12 tuition up to $10,000/year also qualifies
  • Non-qualified distributions: earnings portion is taxable + 10% penalty
  • Rollovers to Roth IRA: Under SECURE 2.0, after 15 years you can roll up to $35,000 lifetime into a Roth IRA for the beneficiary

This non-refundable credit covers expenses paid so you (and your spouse if MFJ) can work or look for work. You can claim 20%–35% of qualifying expenses:

  • Up to $3,000 for one qualifying child/dependent
  • Up to $6,000 for two or more
  • Qualifying persons: children under 13, disabled spouse, or disabled dependent

Use Form 2441. You must include the care provider's name, address, and Taxpayer ID number.

Claim on Form 8863. Requirements:

  • Student must be enrolled at least half-time in the first 4 years of higher education
  • Maximum credit: $2,500 (100% of first $2,000 + 25% of next $2,000 in qualifying expenses)
  • 40% is refundable (up to $1,000)
  • Must have Form 1098-T from the school
  • Student must not have a felony drug conviction
  • Cannot be claimed more than 4 tax years for the same student

The Lifetime Learning Credit offers up to $2,000 per tax return (not per student) — 20% of the first $10,000 in qualifying tuition and fees.

  • No limit on number of years it can be claimed
  • Covers any level of post-secondary education (undergraduate, graduate, professional)
  • Also covers courses to improve job skills — student doesn't need to be pursuing a degree
  • Non-refundable (can reduce tax to zero but not below)
  • Phase-out: $80,000–$90,000 single / $160,000–$180,000 MFJ

You'll receive Form SSA-1099. The taxable portion depends on your "combined income" (AGI + non-taxable interest + 50% of Social Security benefits):

  • Under $25,000 (single) / $32,000 (MFJ): 0% taxable
  • $25,000–$34,000 (single) / $32,000–$44,000 (MFJ): Up to 50% taxable
  • Over $34,000 (single) / $44,000 (MFJ): Up to 85% taxable

Note: You don't pay Social Security taxes on Social Security benefits — this is about federal income tax on those benefits.

An additional 0.9% Medicare tax applies to wages, salaries, and self-employment income above:

  • $200,000 for single filers / heads of household
  • $250,000 for married filing jointly
  • $125,000 for married filing separately

Your employer withholds once your wages from that employer exceed $200,000, regardless of filing status. If there's a discrepancy (e.g., two jobs or MFJ spouses), you reconcile on Form 8959.

A 3.8% surtax on the lesser of: (1) net investment income, or (2) the amount your MAGI exceeds the threshold.

  • Threshold: $200,000 (single) / $250,000 (MFJ) / $125,000 (MFS)
  • Net investment income includes: dividends, interest, capital gains, rental income, passive activity income, royalties
  • Does NOT include wages, self-employment income, Social Security, retirement distributions, or income from active businesses

Calculated on Form 8960.

File Form 4868 by April 15 to get an automatic 6-month extension to October 15.

Important: An extension gives you more time to file, NOT more time to pay. If you owe taxes:

  • Estimate and pay any balance due by April 15 to avoid failure-to-pay penalties (0.5% per month) and interest
  • Failure-to-file penalty is steeper (5% per month, up to 25%) than failure-to-pay penalty
Filing an extension is free and automatic when filed on time. There's no "good reason" required — just file the form.

The AMT is a parallel tax system that runs alongside the regular income tax. You pay whichever is higher. For 2025:

  • AMT exemption: $88,100 (single) / $137,000 (MFJ)
  • Phase-out of exemption begins: $626,350 (single) / $1,252,700 (MFJ)
  • AMT rates: 26% on first $232,600 of AMTI; 28% above

Common AMT triggers: exercising incentive stock options (ISOs), large SALT deductions, accelerated depreciation, and high miscellaneous deductions. Calculated on Form 6251.

File Form 1040-X to correct a previously filed return. Key deadlines:

  • Generally 3 years from the original filing date (or 2 years from date paid, whichever is later) to claim a refund
  • No deadline to file an amended return to pay additional tax (though interest and penalties accrue)
  • Amended returns for tax years 2021 and later can be e-filed
  • Earlier years must be paper-filed

Include all changed forms and schedules. Explain the changes in Part III of Form 1040-X.

Keep supporting documents for at least:

  • 3 years from the filing date — the IRS general audit window
  • 6 years if you underreported income by more than 25%
  • 7 years if you claimed a loss from worthless securities or bad debt
  • Indefinitely if you didn't file, or filed a fraudulent return

Essential documents to retain: W-2s, 1099s, charitable receipts, investment purchase records (to calculate cost basis), home purchase and improvement records, business expense receipts, IRA basis records (Form 8606).

Keep home purchase and improvement records for as long as you own the home plus the retention period, as they affect your gain calculation when you sell.
