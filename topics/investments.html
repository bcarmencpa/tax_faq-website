Investments & Property

Capital gains, real estate, stock options & equity compensation

12 topics

Capital gains tax depends on how long you held the asset:

  • Short-term (held ≤ 1 year): Taxed as ordinary income at your regular marginal rate (10%–37%)
  • Long-term (held > 1 year): Preferential rates of 0%, 15%, or 20%

2025 long-term capital gains rates:

  • 0% — Taxable income up to $48,350 (single) / $96,700 (MFJ)
  • 15% — Up to $533,400 (single) / $600,050 (MFJ)
  • 20% — Above those thresholds

High earners may also owe the 3.8% Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT), bringing the effective top rate to 23.8%.

The wash sale rule disallows a capital loss if you buy a substantially identical security within 30 days before or after the sale that generated the loss (a 61-day window total).

  • The disallowed loss is added to the cost basis of the replacement shares
  • Applies to stocks, bonds, mutual funds, and options
  • Applies across accounts — including IRAs and your spouse's accounts
  • Cryptocurrency is currently NOT subject to the wash sale rule (as of 2025), though legislation has been proposed
To harvest a loss and maintain market exposure, you can buy a similar (but not substantially identical) ETF or fund during the 30-day window.

The IRS treats cryptocurrency as property, not currency. Every taxable event must be reported:

  • Selling crypto for dollars: Capital gain or loss (short-term or long-term)
  • Trading one crypto for another: Taxable — treated as selling the first at fair market value
  • Using crypto to buy goods/services: Taxable at the time of transaction
  • Mining or staking rewards: Ordinary income at fair market value when received
  • Receiving crypto as payment: Ordinary income

Report on Form 8949 and Schedule D. Many exchanges provide tax forms but they may be incomplete — consider crypto tax software (CoinTracker, Koinly, TaxBit).

The IRS requires all taxpayers to answer the digital asset question on Form 1040 — even if you had no transactions.

Rental income is reported on Schedule E and taxed as ordinary income. You can deduct:

  • Mortgage interest, property taxes, insurance
  • Repairs and maintenance (not improvements)
  • Depreciation (27.5 years for residential rental property)
  • Property management fees, advertising, supplies
  • Travel to the property for management purposes

Passive activity rules: Rental losses are generally passive. They can only offset passive income — unless you actively participate (can offset up to $25,000 of ordinary income if AGI is under $100,000, phasing out to $150,000) or qualify as a Real Estate Professional.

Depreciation lets you deduct the cost of an income-producing asset over its useful life. For rental real estate:

  • Residential rental property: Depreciated over 27.5 years using straight-line method
  • Commercial property: 39 years
  • Only the building (not land) is depreciable
  • Personal property within a rental (appliances, carpets): 5 or 7 years

Depreciation recapture: When you sell a rental, prior depreciation deductions are "recaptured" and taxed at up to 25% (Section 1250 unrecaptured gain) — even if you qualify for long-term capital gain rates on the rest of the gain.

A Section 1031 like-kind exchange allows you to defer capital gains tax when you sell investment real estate and reinvest in another qualifying property.

  • Both properties must be held for investment or business use (no personal residences)
  • 45-day identification rule: You must identify replacement property(ies) within 45 days of closing the sale
  • 180-day closing rule: You must close on the replacement property within 180 days
  • Must use a qualified intermediary (QI) — you cannot touch the proceeds
  • To defer ALL gain: reinvest all equity and replace all debt
The deferred gain is eventually taxable when you sell the replacement property (unless you do another 1031) or inherit it (heirs get a stepped-up basis).

Incentive Stock Options (ISOs):

  • No regular income tax at exercise (but the spread is an AMT preference item)
  • If you hold shares >2 years from grant and >1 year from exercise: long-term capital gain on sale
  • If you sell earlier (disqualifying disposition): ordinary income on the spread at exercise

Non-Qualified Stock Options (NSOs / NQSOs):

  • Spread at exercise is ordinary income — reported on W-2 (employees) or 1099 (non-employees)
  • Subsequent gain/loss on the shares is capital gain/loss
  • Cost basis = exercise price + income recognized at exercise
ISO exercise can trigger significant AMT liability. Plan with us before exercising large ISO grants.

Restricted Stock Units (RSUs) are taxed as ordinary income at vesting, based on the fair market value of shares on the vest date.

  • Reported on your W-2 — your employer withholds taxes (usually at the 22% supplemental rate, which may not be enough)
  • Your cost basis for capital gains purposes = FMV on vest date
  • If you sell immediately at vesting: typically little or no capital gain/loss
  • If you hold after vesting: any additional gain/loss is short-term or long-term capital gain from vest date
Many employees under-withhold on RSU income. Check your withholding and adjust W-4 or pay estimated taxes to avoid a surprise bill in April.

Under Section 121, you can exclude up to $250,000 ($500,000 for MFJ) of gain from the sale of your primary residence.

  • You must have owned and used the home as your primary residence for at least 2 of the last 5 years before the sale
  • Can be used once every 2 years
  • Gain above the exclusion is taxable as long-term capital gain (if held >1 year)
  • Home improvements add to your cost basis and reduce your gain
  • If you used part of your home for business, depreciation recapture may apply to that portion
Keep records of all home improvements — they increase basis and reduce your taxable gain when you sell.

U.S. persons are taxed on worldwide income. Key rules for foreign investments:

  • Foreign tax credit (Form 1116): Taxes paid to foreign governments can be credited dollar-for-dollar against U.S. tax on the same income
  • PFICs (Passive Foreign Investment Companies): Foreign mutual funds and ETFs may be PFICs — subject to punitive tax rules. File Form 8621. Consider using U.S.-listed foreign funds instead.
  • Foreign dividends and interest: Reported on Schedule B; may be taxed at ordinary rates even if "qualified" dividends don't meet U.S. requirements
If you have foreign financial accounts, you may also have FBAR and Form 8938 filing requirements. See the FBAR topic below.

The FBAR (FinCEN Form 114) must be filed if you have a financial interest in or signature authority over foreign bank accounts with an aggregate value exceeding $10,000 at any point during the calendar year.

  • Due date: April 15, with an automatic extension to October 15
  • Filed electronically through the BSA E-Filing System (not the IRS)
  • Penalties: Up to $10,000 per violation (non-willful); up to the greater of $100,000 or 50% of account value per violation (willful)
  • Form 8938 (FATCA): Separate IRS filing for higher thresholds ($50,000 for single filers living in the U.S.)
FBAR is separate from your tax return and is often overlooked. If you have foreign accounts, let us know so we can ensure compliance.

You'll receive Form 1099-DIV for dividends and Form 1099-INT for interest. Report on Schedule B if total interest or ordinary dividends exceed $1,500, or if you have foreign accounts.

  • Qualified dividends: Taxed at preferential long-term capital gains rates (0%, 15%, 20%) — must be from U.S. or qualifying foreign corporations, held for minimum period
  • Ordinary dividends: Taxed as regular income
  • Interest income: Fully taxable as ordinary income (except municipal bond interest, which is federal-tax-exempt but may be subject to state tax)
  • Treasury interest: Taxable federally, exempt from state tax
