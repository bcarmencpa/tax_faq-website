Tax Planning & Compliance

Strategies, estate & gift tax, charitable giving, IRS procedures & NY State

19 topics

A Roth conversion makes sense when you expect your tax rate to be higher in the future than it is today. Consider converting when:

  • You're in an unusually low-income year (job loss, early retirement, business loss)
  • The market is down — you convert fewer dollars of value
  • You have losses or deductions to offset the conversion income
  • You're in the 0% or 10%–12% bracket and have room before the next bracket
  • You want to reduce future RMDs (Roth IRAs have no RMDs)
  • You're doing estate planning and want to pass tax-free assets to heirs
There's no one-size-fits-all answer. We can model various scenarios to find the optimal conversion amount for your situation. Call us before year-end.

Tax-loss harvesting is selling investments that have declined in value to realize a capital loss that can offset gains or ordinary income.

  • Capital losses first offset capital gains (same type: short offsets short, long offsets long; then cross)
  • Net capital losses can offset up to $3,000 of ordinary income per year
  • Excess losses carry forward indefinitely to future years
  • Watch the wash sale rule — don't repurchase the same (or substantially identical) security within 30 days
  • You can immediately buy a similar (not identical) fund to maintain market exposure
Best done in taxable brokerage accounts. Losses in IRAs or 401(k)s don't generate deductible losses.

Key above-the-line strategies that reduce AGI (no itemizing required):

  • 401(k) / 403(b) / 457 contributions: Up to $23,500 ($31,000 if 50+) in 2025
  • Traditional IRA contributions: Up to $7,000 ($8,000 if 50+) if deductible
  • HSA contributions: $4,300 self / $8,550 family
  • SEP-IRA or Solo 401(k): Self-employed can shelter up to $70,000 in 2025
  • Half of SE tax is deductible for self-employed
  • Self-employed health insurance premiums are deductible
  • Student loan interest: Up to $2,500
  • Alimony (pre-2019 divorces): Deductible for payer

Reducing AGI also has downstream benefits: it can increase eligibility for credits, reduce Medicare premiums (IRMAA), and lower exposure to the NIIT and Additional Medicare Tax.

You can give up to $19,000 per recipient in 2025 without filing a gift tax return or using any of your lifetime exemption. Key rules:

  • The $19,000 is per recipient — you can give $19,000 to as many people as you want
  • Gift splitting: Married couples can give $38,000 per recipient jointly
  • Gifts above $19,000 per recipient require filing Form 709 and reduce your lifetime exemption ($13.99 million in 2025)
  • Direct tuition and medical payments are completely exempt from gift tax — pay the school or medical provider directly (not the individual)
  • 529 plan contributions: can front-load 5 years ($95,000 per beneficiary) via "superfunding" election

The federal estate tax applies to estates exceeding the exemption amount at a 40% rate.

  • 2025 exemption: $13.99 million per person (made permanent by the OBBBA)
  • Portability: A surviving spouse can elect to use the deceased spouse's unused exemption (DSUE) — effectively $27.98 million for married couples
  • Stepped-up basis: Heirs receive assets at fair market value on date of death — eliminating embedded capital gains built up during the decedent's lifetime
  • New York State: NY has its own estate tax with a $7.16 million exemption (2025) — significantly lower than federal. NY has a "cliff" — if your estate exceeds 105% of the exemption, the entire estate is taxed, not just the excess

To deduct charitable contributions you must itemize. Key rules:

  • Cash donations: Deductible up to 60% of AGI; carry forward excess for up to 5 years
  • Appreciated property (stocks, real estate): Deduct fair market value, up to 30% of AGI; avoid capital gains tax on the appreciation
  • Acknowledgment requirement: Contributions of $250+ require written acknowledgment from the charity
  • Non-cash contributions over $500: File Form 8283; appraisal required for property over $5,000
  • Donor Advised Fund (DAF): Contribute a large amount in a high-income year, take the deduction now, then distribute to charities over time
Donating appreciated stock instead of cash is almost always more tax-efficient — you avoid capital gains and still get the full deduction.

A QCD lets you transfer up to $108,000 per year (2025, indexed) directly from your IRA to a qualified charity — completely tax-free.

  • Must be age 70½ or older to use a QCD
  • The distribution counts toward your Required Minimum Distribution (RMD)
  • The amount is excluded from income entirely — better than a deduction for most people since it reduces AGI directly
  • Lower AGI can reduce Medicare premiums, Social Security taxability, and exposure to phaseouts
  • The check must be made payable to the charity, not to you
  • Does not work with donor-advised funds or private foundations
If you're over 70½, charitably inclined, and have an IRA, the QCD is almost always the best way to give.

Most "audits" are actually correspondence audits — a letter asking you to verify a specific item. Don't panic. Steps to take:

  • Read the notice carefully — understand exactly what the IRS is questioning
  • Gather documentation supporting the item in question
  • Respond by the deadline (usually 30–60 days) — extensions are usually available
  • Never ignore an IRS notice — problems compound quickly
  • Common notices: CP2000 (income mismatch), CP501/503/504 (balance due)
  • Field audits (in-person) are rare; if you receive one, retain a CPA or tax attorney immediately
If you receive any IRS notice, call our office before responding. We represent clients before the IRS and can handle communications on your behalf.

Penalties for non-compliance add up quickly:

  • Failure to file: 5% of unpaid tax per month (up to 25% max) — applies from the filing deadline
  • Failure to pay: 0.5% of unpaid tax per month (up to 25% max)
  • If both apply, failure-to-file is reduced to 4.5% (combined max still 25%)
  • Interest: Accrues on unpaid tax at the federal short-term rate + 3% — currently around 7–8% annually
  • Fraud penalty: 75% of underpayment if fraud is proven

If you can't pay in full, still file on time — it stops the much steeper failure-to-file penalty from accruing. Pay what you can and set up a payment plan for the rest.

The IRS offers several installment agreement options:

  • Short-term payment plan (≤180 days): For balances under $100,000. No setup fee. Apply online at irs.gov.
  • Long-term installment agreement: Monthly payments over up to 72 months. Setup fee of $31 (online/direct debit) to $130. Available if balance is under $50,000.
  • Streamlined installment agreement: Under $50,000 owed; no financial statement required; pay within 72 months

Interest and the failure-to-pay penalty continue to accrue during the agreement, but at a reduced penalty rate of 0.25%/month (vs 0.5%) while in good standing.

We can negotiate installment agreements and other resolutions with the IRS on your behalf. Call us if you have a balance due.

An Offer in Compromise (OIC) allows you to settle your tax debt for less than the full amount owed, if paying in full would create financial hardship. The IRS accepts OICs based on:

  • Doubt as to collectibility: Most common — you can't pay the full amount even selling all assets and using future income
  • Doubt as to liability: You dispute the tax assessment itself
  • Effective tax administration: Paying would be inequitable or create undue hardship

The IRS calculates your "reasonable collection potential" (RCP) based on equity in assets + future income. Your offer must generally equal or exceed the RCP.

OIC acceptance rates are low (~30%). Beware of "tax relief" companies advertising guaranteed settlements — most don't deliver. We provide honest assessments of your options.

New York has its own tax system with several unique features:

  • NY State rates: 4%–10.9% graduated brackets (higher than most states)
  • NYC resident tax: Additional 3.078%–3.876% for NYC residents — one of the highest combined state+city rates in the country
  • NY "decoupling": NY does not always conform to federal tax law — e.g., NY may not allow the same bonus depreciation or QBI deduction limits
  • NY estate tax: $7.16 million exemption (2025) with the "cliff" — if your estate exceeds 105% of the exemption, the entire estate is taxed
  • STAR program: School Tax Relief for primary residences — Basic STAR and Enhanced STAR (for seniors) reduce property taxes
  • Nonresidents: NY taxes income earned in NY even for non-residents — if you work in NY but live in NJ or CT, you file a NY nonresident return
  • Telecommuting / "Convenience of the Employer" rule: NY taxes remote workers if they work from home for their own convenience (not employer necessity)

U.S. persons with foreign financial accounts or assets have two key reporting requirements:

  • FBAR (FinCEN 114): Required if aggregate value of foreign accounts exceeded $10,000 at any point in the year. Due April 15, auto-extended to October 15. Filed at BSA E-Filing System (not IRS).
  • Form 8938 (FATCA): Required if foreign financial assets exceed $50,000 (single, U.S.-based) — higher thresholds apply for MFJ and overseas residents. Filed with your tax return.

These requirements often overlap. Both may apply. Penalties for non-compliance are severe ($10,000+ per violation for FBAR).

The Foreign Account Tax Compliance Act (FATCA) requires U.S. taxpayers to report foreign financial assets on Form 8938 (filed with your tax return).

  • Threshold (single, U.S. resident): $50,000 at year-end or $75,000 at any point during the year
  • Threshold (MFJ, U.S. resident): $100,000 at year-end or $150,000 at any point
  • Higher thresholds for U.S. citizens living abroad
  • Covers foreign bank accounts, stocks/securities in foreign accounts, interests in foreign entities, foreign pension plans
  • FATCA also requires foreign financial institutions to report U.S. account holders to the IRS — the IRS cross-references these reports with Form 8938 filings
FBAR and Form 8938 overlap but are not identical — you may need to file both. The FBAR threshold ($10,000) is much lower than FATCA's.

RMDs are minimum annual withdrawals required from traditional IRAs, 401(k)s, and most other retirement accounts starting at age 73 (75 for those born after 1960 under SECURE 2.0).

  • Calculated by dividing prior year-end account balance by an IRS life expectancy factor
  • Must be taken by December 31 each year (first RMD can be delayed to April 1 of the year after you turn 73)
  • Penalty for missed RMD: 25% of the shortfall (reduced to 10% if corrected promptly)
  • Roth IRAs have no RMDs during the owner's lifetime — Roth 401(k)s now also exempt under SECURE 2.0

Planning strategies: Consider Roth conversions before RMDs begin to reduce future required amounts; use QCDs to satisfy RMDs tax-free if charitably inclined.

High earners who exceed Roth IRA income limits can use the backdoor Roth strategy:

  • Step 1: Make a non-deductible contribution to a traditional IRA ($7,000 / $8,000 if 50+)
  • Step 2: Convert the traditional IRA to a Roth IRA shortly after contributing
  • If done correctly with no pre-tax IRA funds, the conversion is tax-free (you already paid tax on the contribution)

Pro-rata rule: If you have other pre-tax IRA funds (rollover IRAs, deductible IRAs), the conversion will be partially taxable based on the ratio of pre-tax to after-tax IRA money. File Form 8606 to track basis.

The mega backdoor Roth (for high-earning 401(k) participants) allows up to $46,500 in additional after-tax contributions that can be converted to Roth — if your plan allows it.

"Bunching" means concentrating deductions into alternating years to exceed the standard deduction in one year, then taking the standard deduction the next.

  • Example: Give $10,000 to charity each year → you might not exceed the standard deduction either year. Instead, give $20,000 every other year → itemize in giving years, take standard deduction in off years.
  • Works well with charitable giving (especially via a Donor Advised Fund — front-load contributions, distribute over multiple years)
  • Can also bunch medical expenses, property tax prepayments, or other discretionary deductions
  • The higher SALT cap under the OBBBA makes bunching more effective for NY taxpayers

An HSA (Health Savings Account) offers a triple tax advantage — the best of any account type:

  • Tax-deductible contributions (reduce AGI immediately)
  • Tax-free growth (invest contributions like an IRA)
  • Tax-free withdrawals for qualified medical expenses (now and in retirement)

Advanced strategy: If you can afford to pay medical expenses out-of-pocket, let the HSA grow invested. Keep receipts forever — there's no time limit on reimbursements. Withdraw in retirement to cover past medical expenses tax-free, or treat it like a traditional IRA after age 65 (non-medical withdrawals taxable but no penalty).

2025 limits: $4,300 self-only / $8,550 family / +$1,000 catch-up (age 55+)

A year-end tax checklist to review each November–December:

  • Estimate your tax liability — compare to prior year to see if you need to adjust withholding or pay an estimated tax payment
  • Maximize retirement contributions — 401(k) contributions must be made by December 31; IRA contributions can wait until April 15
  • Harvest capital losses — offset gains realized during the year
  • Bunch charitable giving — if you're close to the standard deduction threshold, consider bunching this year or next
  • Review Roth conversion opportunity — if income is lower than expected, fill up your bracket
  • Take RMDs — if applicable, must be done by December 31
  • Max HSA and FSA — FSA use-it-or-lose-it rules apply
  • Business: defer income / accelerate deductions — if on cash basis, delay billing or prepay deductible expenses
Schedule a year-end planning call with us by November 15 — there's still time to implement most strategies before December 31.
